Methodology & Technical Note

How this model calculates what it calculates — the financial mechanics, the impact assumptions, and the things it deliberately does not attempt.

Last revised 2026-08-21. This note is kept in step with the model specification in docs/MODEL_SPEC.md; where the two disagree, the specification is authoritative and this page is out of date.

1. What this model does

It simulates a blended-finance revolving fund that finances market-based sanitation, month by month, over a chosen horizon. Capital flows in three hops:

Investors ──grant──▶ GRANT LEDGER ──subsidy──▶ Households (no repayment) ──loan───▶ LOAN LEDGER ──loan─────▶ Micro-enterprises (repay with interest) ──loan─────▶ Households (repay with interest) LOAN LEDGER ──service──▶ Investors (principal + interest)

It answers three questions, and it is built to answer these rather than to be a general-purpose financial model:

  1. Solvency. Does the fund run out of cash, and when?
  2. Repayment. Is senior debt repaid in full, within its term?
  3. Reach and impact. How many toilets, people, DALYs and tonnes of CO2e, at what cost per unit?

Deliberately out of scope: foreign exchange and multi-currency, taxation, sale of the portfolio to a third party, borrower-level heterogeneity, district-level disaggregation, construction quality, and behavioural adoption dynamics. Adding any of these is a decision to be recorded, not a feature to be slipped in.

2. The two verdicts

After every run the model reports two separate results, and the distinction matters more than any single number on the dashboard.

Verdict Question What a failure means
Ledger integrity Is the arithmetic internally consistent? A defect in the model. Cash has appeared or vanished, a total does not match its components, or a value is not a number. Report it; do not work around it.
Fund viability Does the fund actually work? A finding about your scenario. The model is telling you the fund as specified does not stand up. That is a legitimate and useful result, not an error.

A run is viable only if all four of these hold:

V1 min(cash balance) >= 0 never insolvent V2 principal repaid >= senior loan - $1,000 senior debt repaid in full V3 capitalised interest < $1,000 never had to roll up unpaid interest V4 OSS >= 1.0 operations cover themselves

Why this is called out so prominently

Until 2026-08-20 the model printed “Model Integrity Verified” for its own shipped demonstration scenario — a fund that went insolvent in year four and defaulted on 18.7% of its senior loan. The insolvency and the default were written to the browser console, where nobody sees them. The line that would have failed the run was commented out and labelled “optional strictness”.

A green tick against a failing fund is worse than no check at all, because it stops anyone looking. The two verdicts are now computed separately, shown separately, and never merged into a single “model OK” indicator.

3. The monthly waterfall

Within each month, operations run in this exact order. The ordering is the point: debt and operations are funded before the fund is permitted to lend, which is what makes the solvency gate meaningful.

# Step Effect
1 Retire toilets past their service life Stops carbon crediting for that vintage
2 Micro-enterprise closures Reduces production capacity
3 Inflation and unit cost for the month —
4 Collect household repayments and interest Loan ledger +
5 Collect micro-enterprise repayments and interest Loan ledger +
6 Service investor debt — interest first, then principal Loan ledger −
7 Pay fixed operating costs Loan ledger −
8 Solvency gate — may the fund lend at all? —
9 Micro-enterprise expansion lending Loan ledger −
10 Toilet production — grants first, then loans Grant ledger −, loan ledger −
11 Carbon revenue Grant ledger +
12 Record impact and push the audit trail —

The solvency gate

New lending is permitted only when the fund holds more than its operating reserve:

requiredReserve = 3 months of FULL fixed operating cost (never the hibernation-cut figure) + the next 3 months of scheduled investor principal solvent = loanCash >= requiredReserve AND grantCash >= 0 lendable = solvent ? max(0, loanCash - requiredReserve) : 0

One consequence worth knowing before you read too much into a healthy-looking cash line: the fund lends down to exactly this floor, so minimum cash across a run converges on roughly three months of operating cost plus near-term debt service, almost regardless of how well the fund is doing. Thin headroom is a property of the lending rule, not necessarily a sign of distress.

The debt-service lookahead now exists

Until 2026-08-21 the reserve was 3 × opsCost only — using the ops figure after it had already been cut to 30% during hibernation, so the buffer shrank by 70% exactly when the fund was most fragile, and it ignored investor principal due the next quarter entirely, despite the tool's own documentation claiming that lookahead existed. Both are fixed. On the shipped default scenario, reach fell from 133,469 to 121,358 toilets (−9%) as a direct, predicted consequence: the fund now holds back cash it already knows it owes, instead of lending it away. Minimum cash improved. See docs/adr/0027-debt-service-lookahead-reserve.md.

4. The dual ledger

Two cash pools that never transfer into one another. This is the structure that stops concessional subsidy and repayable capital silently cross-subsidising each other.

Grant ledger Loan ledger
Funded by Initial grant capital, carbon revenue Initial loan capital, all repayments and interest received
Pays for Household subsidies and their variable fees Household loans, micro-enterprise loans, fixed and variable operating costs, investor principal and interest

The headline cash figure is the sum of the two, which means a healthy grant ledger can mask an overdrawn loan ledger in the top-line chart. The CSV export separates them.

Write-offs are not cash. A loan written down reduces the portfolio balance and is reported as a write-off, but no money leaves the building. This is checked on every run: net cash flow is rebuilt from its components and must reconcile.

5. Loan portfolio mechanics

Cohorts, not a blended balance

Every disbursement creates its own cohort with its own balance, payment and remaining term. Cohorts are never merged. This is what makes term structure and run-off behave correctly — a loan made in month 30 matures in month 30 plus its term, not on some averaged schedule.

Payments

payment = rate == 0 ? principal / termMonths : (principal × rate) / (1 - (1 + rate)^(-termMonths))

The zero-rate branch is not decoration. Without it, a 0% concessional loan produces 0/0, and the resulting NaN propagates silently through the entire ledger — passing every consistency check on the way, because NaN comparisons are always false.

Rate conversion

Annual rates convert to monthly geometrically, never by dividing by twelve:

monthlyRate = (1 + annualRate)^(1/12) - 1

The write-down rate is not the share of loans that fail

The field labelled “Annual Write-down Rate” is converted to a monthly hazard and applied to the outstanding balance every month:

monthlyHazard = 1 - (1 - annualRate)^(1/12) writeOff[m] = balance × monthlyHazard

This is a continuous fractional write-down, not a discrete “x% of borrowers default”. Because an amortising balance declines over its term, realised loss as a share of money lent is lower than the headline rate, and the gap depends on the loan term. On a short term the difference is large.

Interest is charged on the post-write-down balance, so a deteriorating loan also earns less. The payment schedule is not re-amortised, so the loss lands on the final payment rather than as a missed one.

6. Investor debt

A single senior loan, drawn in full at the start, with flat principal amortisation over the repayment term after any grace period, plus interest on the outstanding balance.

Grace defers principal, not interest

Interest accrues from month one regardless of grace. A grace period delays principal repayment so that capital can be deployed; it does not make the money free. The model previously gated both on the grace condition, which silently forgave the interest outright.

The fund never pays money it does not have

available = max(0, loanCash) actualInt = min(scheduledInt, available) actualPrin = min(scheduledPrin, max(0, available - actualInt))

Interest ranks ahead of principal.

Arrears capitalise

Interest the fund cannot pay is added to the outstanding balance in the month it is missed, and reported separately as capitalised interest. Unpaid principal is carried forward and caught up automatically once cash allows.

The closing liability is the balance the simulation actually tracked. It previously reconstructed the figure as original loan minus principal repaid, which by construction cannot know about arrears — in a stressed scenario that overstated net assets by more than the original loan.

A longer repayment term can repay less, not more. Principal amortises flat, so stretching the term lowers each payment — but if the schedule now runs past the fund's productive life, the later payments simply never happen and become permanent arrears. The tool's advice engine no longer suggests extending the term, because testing it against the model shows it makes things worse.

7. Growth and constraints

Production capacity

capacity[m] = activeMicroEnterprises[m] × toiletsPerMePerMonth

No learning curve, no seasonality, no supply-chain lag.

Enterprise closure is separate from loan write-down

These are two different events and they have two different parameters:

  • Write-down rate reduces the value of the loan portfolio.
  • Closure rate reduces the number of enterprises, and therefore capacity.

They come apart in both directions: a business can wind down in good order having repaid in full, and a business can fall behind on payments while continuing to build. Deriving one from the other would bake in an equivalence that does not hold. Previously only write-down was modelled, so a 50% write-down rate left production capacity completely untouched.

Demand

backlog[m] = backlog[m-1] × (1 + populationGrowth)^(1/12) - production[m]

The unserved backlog grows. Population growth was previously collected from the user, auto-filled from World Bank data, and used nowhere — so the model showed a fund closing a gap that is in reality widening.

What binds

Each month, production is the tightest of three limits:

capacity = enterprises × toilets per enterprise per month supply demand = outstanding backlog market maxUnits = lendable / fully-burdened unit cost capital

Grants are allocated first, up to the grant support percentage; loans fill the remainder as far as capital allows.

Grant Support % paces subsidy, it does not increase it

Total grant-funded output is capped by the grant ledger, so the affordability limit binds long before the target percentage does. Sweeping this parameter from 5% to 90% — an eighteen-fold change — moves grant-funded toilets by under 4%. What it does change is how fast the grant fund is spent: at 5% it lasts around two years, at 90% around four months.

Treat it as a deployment-rate control. To subsidise more households, add grant capital.

8. Wind-up

The fund is wound up at the first month that opens with nothing left to do:

portfolio == 0 nothing left to collect production == 0 nothing being built lendable == 0 gate shut, no capital to redeploy

From that month, operating costs stop, interest stops accruing, and the ledger is frozen. Closing figures are measured at wind-up, not at whatever horizon happened to be requested.

Without this, a fund that collapsed in year four kept paying a collections team for the remaining sixteen years of a twenty-year run, against no income at all. Reported ending cash was therefore a measure of how long the simulation was left running rather than of how the fund performed — the same fund closed at −$36,351 over five years and −$1,029,034 over twenty. Extending the horizon now changes nothing.

9. Impact methodology

Health — DALYs averted

dalys[m] = activeToilets[m] × householdSize × dalysPerPersonPerYear / 12

Accrued monthly against toilets in service, so a toilet built in month one contributes more over the run than one built in month one hundred. This area-under-the-curve treatment is deliberate.

Time saved

hours[m] = activeToilets[m] × householdSize × hoursPerPersonPerDay × 30 hourValue = (avgAnnualIncome / 2,080) × timeValueFactor

The hourly value is derived from the country's own income, not from a fixed figure. Two reasons:

  • The tool models many countries. A single global constant is right for at most one of them.
  • The hour saved is household time — walking to, queueing for, or managing sanitation — not forgone paid employment. Valuing non-market time below the market wage is standard practice in both WASH and transport cost–benefit analysis. The default factor is 30%.

Check this factor before publishing. The method — derive from local income, discount below the wage — is well founded. The specific 30% is a conventional round number rather than a figure verified against current published guidance. Confirm it against whichever cost– benefit guidance your programme reports against, and set the input accordingly.

It replaces a hardcoded $0.50/hour whose origin nobody could reconstruct. The arithmetic suggests it was Malawi's income per working hour at the full wage rate, frozen into a multi-country tool.

Carbon

tonnes[m] = creditingToilets[m] × tonnesPerToiletPerYear / 12 revenue[m] = tonnes[m] × valuePerTonne × fundShare

Credits accrue annually, against toilets still within their service life. Carbon methodologies issue credits over a finite crediting period, and a toilet past its service life is not abating anything. Service life is an input, defaulting to five years.

Carbon revenue accrues to the grant ledger, on the reasoning that carbon finance is concessional.

Carbon was previously understated by roughly 250,000×

Three unit errors stacked on one input labelled “Tonnes/Yr”: it was divided by 1,000 as though it were kilograms; the fund's share was divided by 100 a second time having already been converted; and the credit was granted once at construction rather than each year of operation.

The component was, in effect, switched off — and switched off in a way that produced a plausible small number rather than a zero, so it read as “carbon does not move the needle” rather than as a defect. For container-based sanitation and biogas models, where carbon revenue is often decisive, this removed the central argument. Corrected, carbon-financed scenarios shift from capital-constrained to capacity-constrained.

Social return

socialValue = (DALYs × valuePerDALY) + (hoursSaved × hourValue) + (carbonTonnes × valuePerTonne) SROI = socialValue / capitalInvested capitalPreservation = netAssets / capitalInvested

SROI measures social value only. Financial performance is reported beside it, never folded in. Previously the DALY term was computed, displayed prominently on the impact card, and then silently excluded from the ratio — so the screen contradicted itself — while ending cash sat inside a social numerator, letting a fund that hoarded capital and built nothing score well.

SROI figures are not comparable to anything produced before 2026-08-20. Three independent changes moved it: the hours-saved formula was corrected, DALYs were brought in, cash was taken out, and the hourly value was re-derived.

Resolved 2026-08-21. Service life now stops DALYs and time-saved credit, the same way it already stopped carbon — a retired toilet no longer averts disease or saves time in the model. At the shipped 5-year default duration this changes nothing, because no toilet reaches 5 years of age within a 5-year run. On a longer run, headline DALY and time-saved figures fall accordingly — that is the fix working as intended, not a regression. See Q13.

10. Parameter dictionary

Every rate is entered as a percentage

Type 40 for 40%, not 0.40. Conversion happens once, when the model reads the form, and nothing downstream guesses at units.

The form previously taught both conventions at once, under two heuristics that contradicted each other: one divided anything above 1 by 100, the other multiplied anything below 1 by 100. So 100% was ambiguous, and any rate above 100% was unrepresentable — a user modelling 150% inflation, entirely plausible in the countries this tool targets, silently got 1.5%.

11. Data sources

Country context is fetched from the World Bank Indicators API. Every fetched value is a starting suggestion and is fully editable.

Population growth SP.POP.GROW Growth of the unserved backlog

Poverty headcount SI.POV.DDAY Suggested grant support level
Political stability PV.EST Cost contingency mark-up

Administrative units come from countriesnow.space. Where an indicator is unavailable the most recent non-null value within five years is used; where none exists, the field keeps its default.

12. Assumptions and limitations

Read this section before quoting any figure from this tool.

What the model is

  • Deterministic. Nothing random happens. There are no shocks, no bad years, no supply disruptions. Every run of the same inputs gives the same answer.
  • Nominal. All figures include inflation. There is no discounting, and no NPV or IRR is produced. Figures from scenarios with different inflation assumptions are not comparable.
  • Single-currency. Labelled USD. No exchange-rate risk.
  • Monthly. Everything happens at month end, in the order set out in §3.

Simplifications worth knowing

  • Inflation raises costs but not household incomes, so affordability silently tightens over a long run.
  • One household, one toilet, once. No repeat or upgrade demand.
  • Enterprise productivity is flat — no learning curve, no seasonality, no supply-chain lag.
  • Enterprise expansion is capped by two hardcoded constants (10% of lendable capital, 10% growth per month) that are not exposed as inputs. The second materially shapes the growth curve.
  • Grants and loans are the only instruments. No results-based finance, no guarantees, no equity.
  • The break-even solver assumes net assets rise monotonically with the interest rate. That holds in most regimes but breaks when capital is tight, where the solver may converge on a non-solution without saying so.

Margin of interpretation

The impact figures rest on three assumptions the model cannot validate: DALYs averted per person, hours saved per person per day, and CO2e abated per toilet. Each is a user input with a plausible default; none is measured. Treat impact outputs as scenario arithmetic, not as evidence.

13. Open questions

These are modelling decisions awaiting the model owner's judgement. They are listed here rather than settled quietly, because each changes what the outputs mean.

Ref Question
Q2 Still open. Is 30% the right share of the wage at which to value saved household time, against current published guidance? The method is settled (§9); only the specific factor needs confirming, against whichever cost–benefit guidance your programme already reports against.

Resolved 2026-08-21, each as its own decision record: Q13 — service life now stops DALYs and time-saved credit, not just carbon (§9). Q3 — Grant Support % stays a flat share of production; means-testing would need a household-level income distribution the model doesn't have. Q9 — the collections floor stops abruptly at wind-up rather than tapering, because a taper rate would be an invented number. See docs/adr/ for each decision's reasoning.

How to check any of this yourself

The model self-verifies on every run against fourteen ledger invariants, and the repository carries a regression suite covering input wiring, those invariants, twenty-two recorded scenarios, and an end-to-end run of the application.

npm test — the suite.
node tools/verify-findings.js — re-runs every measurement behind the audit and reports whether each defect is still present.
